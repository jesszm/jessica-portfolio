Case 01 · Moving marketplace · 2 apps · 2025

Moving day, from both ends

Muvr is a US marketplace for moves, deliveries and junk removal. Every job has two ends: the person who needs to move, and the driver who does the moving. Each end has its own app. Both had their flows, but they didn't work, so I rebuilt the core flows of both apps from scratch, as the only designer on the project.

01

Overview

Same move, two different jobs

A move looks like one event, but it's two very different experiences. For the person moving, it's a stressful day they want off their plate. For the driver, it's work: getting approved, taking the job, getting paid. So Muvr runs two apps, and each one had to be designed for its own person.

User app

For the person moving

Who
Families, students, offices, stores and warehouses
What they want
A fair price, the right truck and no surprises on the day
What got in the way
A booking that asked everything at once, and still missed what mattered
What I designed
Home, search, move types and a booking flow for all eight
Driver app

For the service provider

Who
Movers, haulers and couriers, with their own truck or joining as helpers
What they want
To get approved quickly and start earning
What got in the way
Up to ten requirements with no clear order, and declines with no explanation
What I designed
Sign-in, roles, an onboarding checklist, review states and help

My role

I was the only designer on the project, freelance, and I owned the work end to end: I audited the old app, mapped the journeys again, designed the core flows and their interfaces, built the components and documented everything for handoff.

Muvr's CEO has a background in moving and logistics, so he joined the reviews as the domain expert: truck sizes, how many movers a job needs, what really slows a move down. I brought the proposals, and he tested them against how moves happen in real life.

03

User app · for the person moving

Booking a move should feel like describing your home

The old booking asked for everything at once, and still missed what drives the price: a flight of stairs, a missing elevator, a gate code, a piano. I rebuilt it so people describe the move in their own terms, one small question at a time, and only when it applies to them.

Four decisions that shaped the booking

  1. The question

    Where should booking start? People open a move type to find out what it includes, not to fill in a form.

    The decision

    Right there. The first question sits above the service levels, so reading about a move is already step one.

  2. The question

    Eight move types, eight different flows? That's eight times the screens, and eight ways to get lost.

    The decision

    One flow whose fields adapt: hall and room for a dorm, unit for an apartment, floor for an office, loading dock for a warehouse.

  3. The question

    What really changes the price? Access: stairs, elevators and opening hours, the things people forget to mention.

    The decision

    Ask about access up front, each question behind a switch that only opens when it applies.

  4. The question

    How big a truck, and how many movers? Few customers can tell, and a wrong guess means a second trip.

    The decision

    Let the system suggest both from the size of the place, with a minimum: four movers above 1,500 sq ft. People can size up, never down.

Home and search

The right move in one tap, or one word

  • Moves, deliveries and junk removal share one home, one tab each.
  • Moves are grouped the way people think of them, each with its own illustration.
  • Search understands the need, not just the name: “loading assistance” finds Labor Only.
  • When nothing matches, the empty state says so kindly, with a way back.

Move types

A page for every move type

  • Each move type explains itself in one line, next to its own illustration.
  • Three levels, side by side. Ticks show exactly what Premium and White-Glove add.
  • The rows change with the move: Labor Only talks about floor protection, not transport.
  • “Where are you moving from?” sits above the table, ready to answer.

Booking · steps 1 and 3

One form that knows the building

  • An apartment asks for the unit and the elevator; a warehouse, the floor, the freight elevator and dock slots.
  • Access as switches. Stairs, elevators and time restrictions, each opening its own counter or time picker.
  • A short note carries what no field can, like a gate code or where the sofa goes.

Booking · step 2

Say what's heavy before anyone lifts it

  • First the size of the place: the numbers that choose the truck.
  • Each item carries what the driver needs: dimensions, a weight range and photos.
  • Removing one asks first, so nothing disappears by accident.
04

Driver app · for the service provider

Getting approved should feel like a checklist, not a maze

Before the first job, a driver has a lot to prove: identity, license, vehicle, insurance, a background check, even how they lift and wrap furniture. The old app threw it all at drivers at once. I turned it into a guided path, with one clear next step and an answer for every submission.

Three decisions that shaped onboarding

  1. The question

    How should drivers sign in? They're often on the move, and passwords get forgotten.

    The decision

    With the phone. One number and a six-digit code, and if the number already has an account, the app asks “Is this you?” before creating another.

  2. The question

    Up to ten requirements before the first job. Where do you start?

    The decision

    A dashboard that always recommends one next step, and folds away what's done. Couriers get a shorter list.

  3. The question

    What happens after you submit a document, and how do you know?

    The decision

    Every review has a visible state: in review, accepted or declined. A decline always comes with a report and a retake.

Sign-in and sign-up

A phone number is all it takes to start

  • One field to begin: the mobile number, with the country set by a flag.
  • “Is this you?” A sheet shows the name on the existing account, with a clear yes or no.
  • A six-digit code, then name, verified email, city and the terms.

Roles

Know what a role asks before you pick it

  • A warm welcome that says what's ahead, identity check included.
  • Every role explains itself: the work, the requirements, and driver versus helper.
  • No truck? The mover card says so right away: you can still join as a helper.

Onboarding dashboard

Up to ten requirements, always one next step

  • The ring shows two separate numbers: submitted and approved.
  • One card marked as next: at 60%, it's the certificate of insurance.
  • Each card shows its status, and the optional and completed sections stay folded.

Requirements

Each requirement shaped around its proof

A vehicle is a short form, safety gear is a checklist and skills are switches. Each requirement asks in the way that fits what it proves, with the same header, progress and buttons around it.

After you submit

Every submission gets an answer

Eight of the requirements need a review, and all eight use the same three screens: an illustration and a time frame while waiting, a small celebration when accepted, and a report with a retake button when declined.

Help center

Help that knows where you're stuck

  • Categories follow onboarding itself: roles, requirements, optionals, verification.
  • Articles answer one question each, and ask “Was this helpful?” at the end.
  • When they don't help, a ticket is one tap away, and its status is easy to follow.

Next up: orders, the way drivers already know them

From the Home & Orders board.

The last piece I mapped was the driver's home: accepting and completing jobs. Ride-hailing apps have already taught drivers how that should feel, so the order card borrows their rhythm and changes as the job gets closer.

05

Design system

Two apps, one family

Both apps are built from the same small set of parts, each one drawn with all of its states. A switch in the booking flow behaves like a switch in onboarding, and developers never have to guess what happens next.

Components 6 groups · shared by both apps

Navigation

Where you are, and how to get back.

Inputs

Asking only what's needed, and showing it back.

Selection

Choices you can see all at once.

Feedback

Every action gets an answer.

Actions

One main action per screen.

Cards

Content people scan, compare and open.

Illustrations with a Muvr accent

The characters started as plain black line drawings. I adapted them to the brand: purple strokes, a soft lilac fill and a pale backdrop, and gave each one a job: reviewing, waiting, something's wrong, done.

One purple, used with care

Purple is Muvr's color, and the revamp kept it, but gave it a job. The bright purple marks what moves you forward: the main button, a switch that's on, progress. A deeper purple marks what you've chosen.

Everything else steps back: white cards with soft corners, dark text and gray for hints, so the purple always means something.

  • Action#9506E6
  • Selected#59048A
  • Card#FFFFFF
  • Text#232430
  • Secondary#4F5059
  • Hint#A7A7AC

One thing I'd change

The light gray used for hints and longer explanations reaches only 2.40:1 on white. Fine for a placeholder, not for text people must read, like why a document was declined. A darker gray, #6E6E76, keeps the same quiet tone and reaches 5.05:1.

And one more

Before paying for the background check, the app shows the driver's full Social Security number to confirm it. The last four digits are enough to confirm, and nobody looking over a shoulder gets the whole number.

06

Outcome

Handed off with the whole map drawn

When I handed off, both apps had their core flows designed, with components and states, and the booking journey was mapped end to end for every move type.

The next phase called for a different kind of engagement, a full-time, day-to-day role inside the team. The terms and scope of that setup didn't fit, so we agreed to close my part at that milestone, amicably, with everything documented for the team to build on.

Handed off

  • Booking journey, all eight move types
  • Booking steps 1 to 3, designed for each
  • Labor Only, end to end
  • Driver sign-in, roles and dashboard
  • Up to ten requirements and their states
  • Help center and tickets
  • Orders, mapped with open questions

What I'd measure next

  • User Booking completion, by move type
  • User Where people drop off, step by step
  • Driver Days from sign-up to first job
  • Driver Declined submissions that get retaken

If I picked it up again

The booking would go in front of real customers, and onboarding in front of real drivers, before anything new gets drawn.
